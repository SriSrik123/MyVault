# Introduction to Electric Charge — PHYS-102
**Date:** 2026-03-30
**Source:** Phys102_original

## Overview
This introductory lecture for Physics II covers the fundamental nature of electric charge, Coulomb's Law governing the force between charges, and the distinction between electrical conductors and insulators. The professor emphasizes that charge — like mass and time — is a primitive, undefinable quantity that we understand only through its properties and behavior.

## Key Concepts

### Electric Charge
- Charge is one of the **fundamental quantities** in physics (alongside mass and time) — it cannot be expressed in terms of anything else
- Two flavors: **positive** and **negative**
  - Opposite charges **attract**
  - Like charges **repel**
- Charge is an **intrinsic** property of particles — electrons carry negative charge, protons carry positive charge, neutrons are uncharged
- In everyday objects, positive and negative charges are nearly equal, so net charge appears zero
- Everything in our physical existence involves the movement of charges (neural signals, touch, etc.)

### Units and Fundamental Charge
- Charge is measured in **Coulombs (C)**
- The **elementary charge**: $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C
- Electrons carry charge $-e$; protons carry charge $+e$
- You will often work in **microcoulombs (μC)** in practice problems

### Coulomb's Law
The force between two point charges $Q_1$ and $Q_2$ separated by distance $r$:

$$F = k \frac{|Q_1||Q_2|}{r^2}$$

Where:
- $k = 8.99 \times 10^9 \ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$ (Coulomb's constant)
- $k$ is also written as $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}$
- The **direction** of the force is along the line connecting the two charges (attractive or repulsive depending on signs)
- In vector form: $\vec{F}_{12} = k\frac{Q_1 Q_2}{r^2}\hat{r}_{12}$ where $\hat{r}_{12}$ is the unit vector from charge 1 to charge 2

Key scaling: **doubling the distance reduces the force by a factor of 4** (inverse square law)

### Atomic Model (Simplified)
- **Nucleus**: extremely dense core containing protons (+) and neutrons (0)
- **Electrons**: orbit the nucleus at relatively large distances — most of the atom is empty space
- The nucleus of a football stadium would be the size of a pea, with electrons at the edges

### Conductors vs. Insulators

**Conductors (metals):**
- Outermost electrons are only weakly bound to their nucleus (due to distance + Coulomb's Law)
- When atoms pack together in a metal, these outer electrons become **free electrons** — communal, shared across the whole material
- Example: copper — the valence electron can jump freely from atom to atom
- Free electrons allow charge to flow → electrical conduction

**Insulators:**
- Electrons are tightly bound to their atoms; they cannot move freely through the material
- Example: paper, glass, rubber

**Dielectrics (special insulators):**
- Electrons don't flow freely but the molecules can **polarize** (stretch/deform in an electric field)
- The positive and negative ends of the molecule shift, creating a dipole effect
- Example: water, certain ionic crystals (NaCl)

### Charge Transfer
- Charge can be transferred between objects by **contact** (conduction)
- Two identical conducting spheres touched together share charge equally
- Demonstrated in class: two charged balls with same charge repel; charged rod attracts paper (polarization/induction)

## Important Formulas / Definitions

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| $q, Q$ | Electric charge (Coulombs) |
| $e$ | Elementary charge = $1.6 \times 10^{-19}$ C |
| $k$ | Coulomb's constant = $8.99 \times 10^9$ N·m²/C² |
| $\epsilon_0$ | Permittivity of free space = $8.85 \times 10^{-12}$ C²/N·m² |
| $F$ | Electrostatic force (Newtons) |
| $r$ | Distance between charges (meters) |

**Coulomb's Law:** $F = k\dfrac{|Q_1||Q_2|}{r^2}$

**Coulomb's constant:** $k = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \approx 8.99 \times 10^9 \ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$

## Summary
Physics II is fundamentally about electric charge — how it behaves at rest (electrostatics) and in motion (currents, magnetic fields). Charge is a primitive quantity understood only by its effects: like charges repel, opposites attract, with a force governed by Coulomb's inverse-square law. The distinction between conductors and insulators arises directly from how tightly bound electrons are within atoms, which itself follows from Coulomb's Law. Prerequisites from Physics I: vectors, free-body diagrams, and Newton's laws will be heavily used throughout this course.
